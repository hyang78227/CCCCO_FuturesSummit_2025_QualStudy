Based on the provided session transcripts, here are the missing elements identified across the conference sessions:

1.  **Sustainable Funding and Planning Models for AI Tools**
    *   **Description:** While the immediate implementation of AI tools (like chatbots and fraud detection) was discussed, there is a lack of concrete detail regarding long-term financial sustainability and planning processes to pay for these technologies once temporary funding expires.
    *   **Condition(s) Met:** 1 (Audience explicitly asked; response lacked actionable long-term detail).
    *   **Evidence:** In **Cam A Session 1**, an audience member asked, "What type of planning processes are you embedding to ensure that some of these practices are sustainable? ...The dollars matter." A panelist responded, "I think those tend to be higher-up decisions... I think that's probably you." Another panelist suggested, "we used COVID funding to do the pilot," and "negotiate that, too, if it's something new."
    *   **Importance:** As colleges move past pilot phases often funded by grants or one-time monies (like COVID relief), administrators need specific frameworks for integrating recurring software costs into general operating budgets to prevent the abandonment of successful tools.

2.  **Strategies for Non-Traditional and Displaced Workers**
    *   **Description:** The sessions heavily favored strategies for Gen Z and traditional-age students. There was a noted absence of specific strategies or curricula designed for older, non-traditional learners or workers already in the workforce who face immediate displacement by AI.
    *   **Condition(s) Met:** 1 (Audience explicitly asked; panel admitted the gap).
    *   **Evidence:** In **Cam A Session 2**, an audience member asked, "What about the nontraditional students? ...we're also worried about the ones that we're not seeing yet who maybe in danger of losing their job." Panelist Christian responded, "I'll be like directly honest like we haven't done much work with unconventional learners." Panelist Tara added, "I really, really do think more in depth work needs to be done there."
    *   **Importance:** Community colleges serve a significant population of adult learners; lacking a strategy for this demographic ignores a large segment of the workforce vulnerable to AI automation.

3.  **Institutional-Level Data Governance Models**
    *   **Description:** While syllabus-level policies (for faculty) were covered in depth, there was a lack of specific models, templates, or "best practices" for *institutional* data governance—specifically regarding how colleges should manage employee use of AI and data privacy at an administrative level.
    *   **Condition(s) Met:** 1 (Audience asked; response was high-level) & 4 (Strong examples for syllabus policy, weak for institutional policy).
    *   **Evidence:** In **Cam D Session 2**, an audience member asked, "Are there best practices? Are there models out there for institutions? Because we are just piecemealing different pieces... guide teachers, but also our overall administrative structures." The panelist response referred generally to a list of policies compiled by an external individual (Joe Sabado) but did not provide specific governance frameworks or examples during the session.
    *   **Importance:** Without centralized governance models, colleges risk data breaches or inconsistent application of tools across departments, creating liability and confusion among staff.

4.  **Technical Implementation of Data Standardization**
    *   **Description:** Multiple sessions identified "messy" or "siloed" data as a primary barrier to implementing AI solutions, but no session provided a technical roadmap or specific implementation steps for how colleges with different legacy systems (ERPs) can achieve the necessary standardization.
    *   **Condition(s) Met:** 3 (Topic appeared across multiple sessions but discussed only at a high level).
    *   **Evidence:** In **Cam D Session 4**, a student presenter noted, "if there's one thing that I want community colleges... to focus on is, standardizing the data... it's like really hard to connect these." In **Cam A Session 1**, panelists mentioned using a "data lakehouse" and "feeding it historical data", but did not explain the technical process of cleaning or integrating that data across incompatible systems.
    *   **Importance:** AI tools rely on quality data; without a clear technical path to standardize data across the 116 colleges, the proposed AI solutions (like career matching or fraud detection) cannot scale effectively.

5.  **Pedagogical Strategies for Student "Existential Anxiety"**
    *   **Description:** Sessions acknowledged that students feel "existential anxiety" regarding the value of their degrees and future careers due to AI. However, specific pedagogical techniques or counseling interventions to address this *emotional* and *philosophical* crisis were missing, with responses reverting to general "literacy" or "safe spaces."
    *   **Condition(s) Met:** 1 (Audience asked; response was general).
    *   **Evidence:** In **Cam C Session 5**, an audience member asked about Gen Z reporting "intense, almost existential anxiety... what is the value of my [work]." The panelist response focused on framing AI tools as a "safe private space to heal," rather than addressing how to restructure curriculum or counseling to directly combat the fear of professional obsolescence.
    *   **Importance:** If students believe their education is rendered obtaining by AI, retention may drop; educators need specific language and curriculum adjustments to validate the human utility of their learning beyond just "using the tools."
